Welcome! Today we ask one simple question: is there enough room for all the wires on a chip?
Imagine the chip is a city. Every wire is a car, and each tile of the grid is one city block.
Start with one block. This road has 2 lanes. Two lanes is its capacity.
Wires arrive like cars. Car 1 fits. Car 2 fits. Now the road is full.
Car 3 has no lane! Demand is 3, capacity is 2, so 3 − 2 = +1. That is a traffic jam.
Real chips are cut into a grid of tiles called GCells. Each one keeps two numbers: demand and capacity.
This tile holds cells A to F. Its demand is 3.5 but its capacity is only 2.0, so it is oversubscribed.
Picture each tile as a bucket. Capacity is the line the water must not cross.
Demand 1.0: plenty of room. Demand 2.0: full. Demand 3.2: it spills over!
surplus = demand − capacity. Zero or below is safe. Above zero means overflow.
Now check the whole chip. We visit every tile and work out its surplus.
Most tiles stay calm and blue. One tile turns yellow: 3.5 − 2.0 = +1.5.
Before you trust a heat map, list the oversubscribed tiles. Here there is just one.
What if the road gets narrower? Capacity is a knob, so let's turn it from 2.0 down to 1.0.
Same wires, same tiles. Watch the borderline tiles turn yellow one by one.
Lower capacity means more traffic jams. That is why capacity is part of the contract.
Your turn! Capacity is 2.0. Demands are 1.4, 2.0, 2.6 and 3.0.
How many are oversubscribed? Think for three seconds.
Two! 2.6 and 3.0 are over. 2.0 is full, not over.
In code it is just three lines: work out the surplus, keep tiles above zero, count them.
Notice the strict “greater than”. A tile that is exactly full is not oversubscribed.
Watch out for three beginner traps.
One: compare wires with wires. Write your units down.
Two: a zero tile beside hot tiles is not automatically safe.
Three: reset the starter placement when you change capacity.
